My approach

How I approached the problem

In shortI started from one question, let cricket sense suggest ideas, and let the data decide which ideas stayed.

  1. 1
    The question

    What does "when it matters" really mean?

    My first idea was simple rules: death overs, a high required rate, knockouts. But a team needing 15 an over with two wickets left is not under pressure. They have already lost.→ Pressure had to measure what is at stake, not how dramatic the scoreboard looks.

  2. 2
    The idea

    Pressure = how much one ball can change the result

    I built a model of each team's chance of winning before every ball, then measured how much that chance could swing on the next ball. Tight, live games score high; decided games score low.→ Needing 6–8 an over with 1–3 wickets left carries 5.7× normal pressure; needing 15+ carries only 0.3×.

  3. 3
    Judging fairly

    Compare like with like

    An opener and a finisher face very different jobs. So every ball is compared with what an average player would do in exactly that spot, and a wicket counts as the runs it costs the team.→ A player's score is the value they add above that bar.

  4. 4
    The surprise

    Pressure-only numbers are mostly luck

    When I split each player's matches into two halves, their pressure-only numbers barely agreed (r = 0.05). Their overall level agreed far more (r = 0.43).→ So I start from each player's overall level and adjust it by their pressure record. A few lucky innings cannot top the list.

  5. 5
    Cricket instinct, tested

    The squeeze the scoreboard does not show

    I suspected that a run of dot balls, a recent collapse and a new batter at the crease add pressure. The data agreed: batters score about 9–13 runs per 100 balls fewer than expected in these moments. My idea that the ground matters failed its test, so I dropped it.→ Only ideas that passed a test set in advance made it into the model.

  6. 6
    Stress test

    Would the answer survive different choices?

    I re-ranked every player 11 different ways, moved the eligibility line to 100 and 200 balls, ran an IPL-only check, and tested whether past ratings predict later seasons.→ The top of both lists held: average robustness 84% for batters and 91% for bowlers.

Where it led. The final lists rank players who add the most value above an average player when the match is on the line, and keep doing it across many games. Suryakumar Yadav and Nathan Ellis lead the batting and bowling lists.

1 · Pressure definition

What counts as pressure?

In shortA ball is high-pressure when its result can change who wins the match, and it counts for more when the batting side is also being squeezed.

Part 1 · Match pressure

We built a model that gives each team's chance of winning before every ball, from the score, wickets, balls left and target. A ball's Pressure Index is how much that chance could swing on that one ball. This covers everything the brief mentions:

  • Required run rate and runs needed: tight chases score high.
  • Wickets in hand: fewer wickets, more pressure.
  • Balls left and stage of the innings: pressure rises at the end of close chases.
  • How close the game is: a match already won or lost scores low.
  • Knockouts (qualifiers, eliminators, semi-finals, finals) count 25% more.
Chase situationPressureMeaning
Need 6–8 an over, 1–3 wickets left5.7×Extreme: one ball can decide it
Need 8–10 an over, 4–6 wickets left2.9×High
Need under 6 an over, 7–10 wickets left0.2×Low: chase under control
Need 15+ an over, 1–3 wickets left0.3×Low: game almost lost
× = times the pressure of an average ball.

2 · Performance measurement

How we score a player

In shortWe compare what a player did on each ball with what an average player would have done in exactly the same situation.

Step 1Clean the data

Fix extras, rebuild scores, remove super overs and washouts.

Step 2Chance of winning

A model predicts who wins from any score, wickets and balls left.

Step 3Find pressure

Balls where that chance can swing most, plus squeeze moments.

Step 4Set the bar

What would an average player do in exactly this spot?

Step 5Score the player

Actual result minus the bar, with wickets counted as runs.

Step 6Rank fairly

Adjust for sample size, check consistency, rank 11 ways.

A worked example

A batter comes in needing 9 an over with 4 wickets left. In that spot an average player would score about 25 runs off 20 balls. Our batter scores 30 off 20 and gets out no more often than average.

That is +5 runs in 20 balls, or a Pressure Score of +25 per 100 balls for that innings.

Getting out costs runs too. A wicket is worth about 8 runs on average (more early in an innings, less at the end), so a cheap dismissal pulls the score down.

Bowlers are scored the same way in reverse: runs saved compared with average, with wickets credited. Run-outs are not credited to bowlers.

Technical detail
Batter value per ball = (runs − expected runs) − wicket cost × (out − expected out) Bowler value per ball = (expected conceded − conceded) + wicket cost × (wicket − expected wicket) Expected values: gradient-boosting models using innings, over, wickets, balls left, run rate needed, IPL vs T20I, season, pace vs spin, batting position, Pressure Index and the three squeeze signals. League average under pressure ≈ 0 (bat -0.63, bowl +0.51 per 100 balls).

3 · Fair comparisons

Keeping it fair

In shortSmall samples, different roles, different competitions and weaker opponents are all adjusted for, so the ranking rewards real skill rather than luck or circumstance.

ProblemWhat we did
A few lucky innings could top the listPlayers with less data are pulled towards average until they have proved themselves (sample-size adjustment, also called shrinkage).
Who is eligible?At least 150 pressure balls faced or bowled since 2022, which is about 25–30 pressure innings. 98 batters and 107 bowlers qualify.
Openers and finishers face different jobsEvery ball is judged against an average player in the same over, wickets, target and batting position.
Walking in after a collapse is harderThe "average player" bar also knows about the three squeeze situations, so nobody is punished for being thrown in at a bad moment.
IPL and internationals score differentlyBoth are used, but each ball is judged against its own competition and season. An IPL-only check gives almost the same top 10.
Runs against weak teams are easierBalls against associate nations count 60% and against A-teams 75%; Test nations and IPL sides count 100%.
Which seasons count?All seasons count equally, because the question is who performed best under pressure across the data. Giving recent seasons extra weight is one of our 11 robustness checks.
One big innings could carry a playerWe also count the share of pressure games in which they came out ahead.

Why is Vaibhav Sooryavanshi not in the top 10? His numbers are outstanding: a Pressure Score of +22.5, higher than anyone in our top 10, and a strike rate of 249 under pressure. But he has faced only 85 high-pressure balls (12 innings) since his debut in 2025, below our 150-ball line, so there is not yet enough evidence to rank him fairly against players with several seasons. We tested moving the line to 100 or 200 balls: only one name changes in each top 10, and he would still not qualify at 100. He is a player to watch as his record grows.

Does the past predict the future?

We rated players on 2022–24 only and checked how they did under pressure in 2025–26.

Of our 2022–24 top 10, still above average in 2025–26Expected by pure chanceVerdict
Bowlers7 of 10about 6a little better than chance
Batters5 of 10about 6hard to predict

What this means. Bowlers who handled pressure well were only a little more likely to keep doing it (correlation +0.16). Batting under pressure swings a lot from season to season, and no measure we tested (ours, strike rate or raw pressure numbers) could predict it. That is why the batting ranking relies on several seasons, consistency and the 11-way average rather than one hot streak. Our lists describe who has performed best under pressure in 2022–26; they are not a forecast.

Checks we ran

  • Win-probability model tested on matches it never saw: chase AUC 0.90, where 0.5 is a coin flip and 1.0 is perfect.
  • Rebuilt scores match official results to within 1 run (median).
  • Fair baseline: an average player scores ≈ 0 under pressure, so the scores are not inflated.
  • Squeeze signals kept only after passing a test set in advance (Section 1).
  • Confidence: 10 of 10 batters and 10 of 10 bowlers are above average with 80% confidence.
  • Robustness: ranked 11 ways plus an IPL-only check (table above).

Where the model is less certain

  • Pressure form is volatile from season to season, especially for batting, so the lists describe past performance rather than predict the future.
  • First-innings pressure is measured less sharply than chase pressure (AUC 0.69 vs 0.90).
  • Bye and leg-bye run amounts are missing from the data; each counts as 1 run.
  • Not included: fielding, wicket-keeping, captaincy, pitch conditions.
  • Smaller samples have wider ranges; Jacob Duffy, Harry Brook, Andre Russell have the fewest pressure balls in our top 10s.

What we learned along the way

Tense is not the same as pressure

A chase needing 15+ an over looks dramatic but carries only 0.3× normal pressure: the game is gone. Needing 6–8 an over with 1–3 wickets left carries 5.7×.

Pressure costs wickets, not runs

Under pressure batters score at about the same rate (strike rate 139 vs 140) but get out about 10% more often.

The squeeze is real

After 3 dot balls in a row, batters score about 11 runs per 100 balls fewer than the situation alone predicts.

Clutch magic is rare

Players who are good under pressure are mostly players who are simply good and do not drop off. True "rises to the occasion" effects are small.

The future is hard to call

7 of our 2022–24 top-10 bowlers stayed above average in 2025–26 (about 6 by chance), and 5 of the batters (about 6 by chance). Past pressure form is only a weak guide to the next season.

Most pressure is in the chase

54% of high-pressure balls come in the second innings, spread across powerplay (32%), middle (41%) and death (28%).

Appendix

Key terms, full tables and data notes

Key terms

Pressure Index
How much one ball can swing the chance of winning, compared with an average ball. Also called leverage.
Squeeze signals (context signals)
Moments of extra strain the scoreboard does not show: a run of dot balls, a recent collapse, a new batter.
Win probability
A model's estimate of a team's chance of winning from the current score, wickets, balls left and target.
Expected runs / wickets
What an average player would produce in exactly the same situation: the context-adjusted baseline.
Pressure Score (run value above expected)
Runs added (or saved, for bowlers) per 100 pressure balls compared with that baseline, with wickets converted to runs.
Rise or dip (clutch factor)
Pressure Score minus the player's own normal score: do they get better or worse under pressure?
Good pressure games (consistency)
Share of a player's pressure innings in which they came out ahead.
Robustness (sensitivity analysis)
Re-ranking with changed assumptions to check the result holds.
Sample-size adjustment (empirical Bayes shrinkage)
Pulling small-sample ratings towards average so lucky streaks do not dominate.
Likely range (bootstrap confidence interval)
Re-sampling a player's matches 500 times to see how much the score could vary.
Forward test (predictive validation)
Rating players on older seasons and checking against newer ones.
AUC
How well a model separates wins from losses: 0.5 = coin flip, 1 = perfect.

Data cleaning

  • 200,999 raw balls; 38 super-over balls and all washed-out matches removed, leaving 843 matches.
  • The runs column is bat runs only, and extras1_runs is a code for the type of extra, not a run count. Team scores were rebuilt and match official results to within 1 run.
  • Beamers and other illegal deliveries are treated as no-balls; wides and no-balls do not count as balls faced.
  • Rain-shortened matches use the revised overs and DLS targets.
  • Each player is tracked by ID with one spelling of their name. Columns with unreliable values (ball speed, free-text shot names) were not used.
